## 18 months to 3 years: when to start toilet training

The sources do not agree on when to start.
NHS Best Start in Life (England, 2026) says almost all children can be potty trained between 18 months and 2 and a half years. It says you do not need to wait for signs that your child is ready before you start.
The AAP says children in general show signs of bladder and bowel control between 18 and 24 months. It says the average age to start in the United States is between 2 and 3 years. It says training can begin as soon as parents and children want to start.
The CDC 18-month tips say: "Most children are not successful at toilet training until 2 to 3 years old." The CDC suggests you ask your child's doctor or teachers if your child is ready.

## 18 months to 3 years: signs a child may be ready (AAP)

The AAP lists these signs that a child may be ready to begin:
- dry for at least 2 hours at a time in the day, or dry after naps
- shows signs of an oncoming pee or poo, such as grunting, freezing or squatting
- can follow simple instructions
- can walk to and from the bathroom and help to undress
- does not like wet nappies and wants a change
- asks to use the toilet or potty chair
- asks to wear "big-kid" underwear
The NHS Best Start in Life page says a child may not tell you when they are wet or need a wee or poo, and that this is completely normal.

## 18 months to 3 years: how to help (NHS Best Start in Life)

Start with daytime training. Keep nappies for naps and for the night at first.
Stop all daytime nappies, including pull-ups, and use pants. Pants help a child notice when they are wet or dry.
Offer the potty or toilet at regular times: after waking, after meals, before going out, before naps and before bed.
Praise each try, whether or not a wee or poo comes.
Accidents are normal. Stay calm, clean up together and move on.
Check for constipation first. The NHS says a child should do soft poos at least every other day. If poos are hard or the child strains, talk to a health visitor or GP before you stop nappies.

## 4 years: daytime control

The AAP says most children in the United States are bowel and bladder trained by 4 years of age.

## Under 5 years: night-time dryness

NHS Best Start in Life says most children learn to stay dry at night after they are dry in the day, and that this happens with time.
The NHS bedwetting page says many children under the age of 5 wet the bed (see the file nhs-bedwetting).

## When to put toilet training on hold (AAP)

The AAP says it can help to wait if the family has just moved or will move soon, has a new baby or expects one, or has a major illness, a death or another crisis.
Talk to your child's doctor if you have any concern before, during or after toilet training.

## Strength of the evidence

These are expert and government advice pages. The pages do not cite studies. The NHS (2026) page is newer than the AAP (2022) page. The difference in start age is real: the NHS says start between 18 months and 2 and a half years; the CDC says most children are not successful until 2 to 3 years.
