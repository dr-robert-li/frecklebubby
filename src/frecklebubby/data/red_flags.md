# Red flags a tracker can detect

Retrieved 2026-11-20. Each threshold is quoted or closely paraphrased from the page named in the Source column; nothing here is from memory. Where sources differ, all versions are listed (see "Differences between sources"). These are warning signs for the app to show with a citation and a "contact a doctor" instruction. They are not a diagnosis. Temperatures are as the source states them (the NHS writes "38C").

## 3. Dehydration / fewer wet nappies

| Threshold as the source states it | What the source says to do | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "has not had a wet nappy in the past 8 hours" (baby crying constantly) | Call 999 | NHS, "Soothing a crying baby", URL above |
| "has had no wet nappies or has not peed for at least 12 hours"; "has not had a pee in the past 12 hours" (immediate action list) | Signs of serious illness; call 999 or go to A&E | NHS, "When to get urgent medical help for babies and children under 5", URL in section 1 |
| Signs of dehydration in a baby: "a sunken soft spot (fontanelle)", "sunken eyes", "few or no tears when they cry", "fewer wet nappies than usual", "being drowsy or irritable" (no number of nappies) | Urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for "fewer wet nappies" | NHS, "Dehydration", https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dehydration/ |
| "hasn't had a wet nappy for 4 to 6 hours"; "has not needed to urinate (wee) in the past 6 to 8 hours"; "has been vomiting (being sick) for more than 24 hours" | "See your doctor right away or go to the nearest emergency department if your baby" has these signs (the page lists more) | Pregnancy, Birth and Baby, "Dehydration in babies", https://www.pregnancybirthbaby.org.au/babies/health-and-wellbeing/dehydration-in-babies |
| "fewer wet nappies or nappies that are not as wet as usual" (mild); sunken fontanelle in severe dehydration (no number) | See page | healthdirect, "Dehydration", https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/healthy-living/food-and-nutrition/dehydration |
| "less wet nappies or not going to the toilet often, dark yellow or brown urine (pee), or dry lips and mouth" (no number) | Speak to a doctor | RCH Kids Health Info, "Gastroenteritis (gastro)", https://www.rch.org.au/kidsinfo/fact_sheets/gastroenteritis_gastro/ |

Expected wet nappies (a baseline for "fewer than usual", not a red-flag threshold):
- NHS, "Formula milk: common questions" (https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding/formula-milk-questions/): "around 6 wet nappies a day from a few days after the birth".
- Australian Breastfeeding Association, "Feeds and nappies in the first week" (https://www.breastfeeding.asn.au/resources/feeds-and-nappies-first-week): at least 1 wet nappy on day 1, 2 on day 2, 3 on day 3, 4 on day 4 and "at least 5 on day five and each day after that"; "at least 5 wet disposable, or 6 very wet cloth nappies in 24 hours".
- AAP HealthyChildren, "Baby's First Days: Bowel Movements & Urination" (https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/babys-first-days-bowel-movements-and-urination.aspx): newborn pink/brick-red urine stain is usually concentrated urine; "As long as the baby is wetting at least four diapers a day, there probably is no cause for concern."

## 6. Other signs the NHS and other pages give that a tracker can partly detect

- Cold/blue/grey/pale/blotchy skin, floppy or hard to wake, no interest in feeding, breathing difficulty: NHS urgent-help and fever pages (URLs above). A tracker usually cannot measure these; the app can only show them as questions.
- NHS: "has not had a drink in the past 8 hours when they've been awake" and "is a baby and is not feeding" (urgent-help page, signs of serious illness).
- NHS, "Colic" and "Reflux in babies" give their own "see a GP" lists (not repeated here).

## Differences between sources

1. Fever wording at under 3 months: NHS, HealthyChildren and Pregnancy Birth and Baby say "38C or higher" (HealthyChildren: 100.4°F / 38.0°C or higher). RCH and the healthdirect warning box say "above 38°C". healthdirect elsewhere defines fever as "38°C or higher", so a reading of exactly 38.0 is treated differently by different pages. A tracker that uses "38.0 or higher" is the stricter choice and matches the NHS, HealthyChildren and PBB wording.
2. Action at under 3 months: NHS says call 111 (fever page) or 999/A&E (urgent-help page); healthdirect and PBB say go to the nearest hospital emergency department immediately; HealthyChildren says call the doctor right away; RCH says see a doctor or health professional, and go to hospital only if under 1 month.
3. Age bands: RCH has a separate under-1-month rule. NHS has a 3-to-6-month rule (39C or higher) that the other pages do not give.
4. Wet nappies: NHS gives 8 hours (crying baby page) and 12 hours (urgent-help page) with no wet nappy; PBB gives 4 to 6 hours without a wet nappy, and 6 to 8 hours without urinating. The sources do not state the same period; the NHS 8-hour figure is for a baby who is also crying constantly. The age to which each period applies is not stated on these pages (the app's "8 h under 6 months" rule is therefore the app's own choice, citing NHS).
5. Low temperature: only the NHS gives a number (below 36C, re-check twice within 10 minutes).
6. Stool: HealthyChildren treats black stool as a concern after the first meconium stools; the NHS pages checked do not give a rule for black stool (NHS lists black vomit, not stool). White or pale stool is flagged by both HealthyChildren (liver problem) and the NHS (jaundice).
